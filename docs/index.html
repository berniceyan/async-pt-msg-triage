Patient messages, triaged by an agent

Before a physician sees it, an LLM agent asks one question of every patient-portal message: can this safely wait three days? On a held-out test set it sends 68 of 70 urgent messages where they need to go.

Every message goes to one of three places: urgent escalation for anything that cannot safely wait three days, the physician queue, where a doctor replies asynchronously three or more days later, or self-care, an automated reply with guidance and warning signs to watch for that no clinician needs to read.

Ask the question that matters, first

Triage is not a classification problem. The errors are not the same size: an urgent message left in a three-day queue is not offset by two prescription refills routed correctly.

So the agent does not start by diagnosing. It starts with the decision a portal actually has to make, asked in plain words: can this message safely wait for a physician’s reply? The answer is one of three words, not a probability, for a reason explained under why probabilities did not work.

Nothing can lower an escalation

The judgment is never called on an escalated message, so it cannot argue one back down.

Failure fails toward safety

A screen that cannot answer escalates. A judgment that cannot answer goes to the physician queue. Neither reaches self-care.

Only one path clears a message

Safe to wait, then no clinician action. Every other combination keeps a clinician in the loop.

Unsure escalates

The system cannot ask a follow-up question, so a message that does not settle whether it can wait is treated as one that cannot.

Where the screen draws the line

The screen’s instructions spell out what can and cannot wait three days. The test is simple: would waiting until day four make things worse than acting on day one?

Escalate now when
  • the problem is getting worse, or is already severe
  • there are signs of spreading infection, heavy bleeding, trouble breathing, or new weakness or numbness
  • a treatment window is closing, like a wound that needs stitches
  • pregnancy, a recent procedure or a weak immune system makes a new symptom riskier
  • there are thoughts of self-harm, or a possible overdose or poisoning
It can wait when
  • it needs a visit, test or prescription, but won’t be worse in three days
  • it has been stable or slowly changing for weeks
  • it is a question about results or a medication

Two extra rules stop the most common mistakes:

  • Silence is not reassurance. If a patient doesn’t mention a warning sign, that doesn’t mean it isn’t there.
  • “Can’t rule it out” is not enough to escalate. Almost nothing can be ruled out from a message alone, so that by itself is no reason to answer unsure.

What came before

The LLM urgency screen is the third design. The first two each failed in a way that pointed to the next.

  1. A keyword and semantic-retrieval screen

    A deterministic screen ahead of the model, with no LLM call. One list of guideline-cited red-flag criteria, each with the phrasings a patient might use, fed two recognizers behind negation and experiencer guards, so “no chest pain” or “my dad has chest pain” would not fire. The first was a keyword matcher that needed the literal phrase. It almost never fired, because patients rarely use the phrase on the list: 4 escalations in 362 messages, and only one of them urgent.

    The second was semantic retrieval, which embedded each sentence with a local encoder and escalated when it came close enough to a criterion. It reached the paraphrases and caught half the urgent messages, but it could not tell a worrying sentence from one that merely sounded like one, and escalated 87 that were not urgent. It was removed.

  2. Judgment only, with probability thresholds

    The model wrote a differential for each message, with a probability and a timeline for every diagnosis that could not be missed, and a message escalated when any probability crossed a threshold. The thresholds were tuned on a development set with only a handful of urgent messages near the line, and did not survive held-out data. The ranking held; the cutoffs did not, and half the urgent messages were missed. This design is kept in the code as the baseline.

  3. An LLM urgency screen, asked first

    Rather than infer urgency from probabilities, ask for it directly: can this safely wait three days? The judgment step stayed, but it now only decides between the physician queue and self-care. Urgent misses fell from 34 of 70 to 2; the price is more non-urgent messages escalated.

Why probabilities did not work

The baseline’s probabilities were good at ranking. Picking one urgent and one non-urgent message at random, the urgent one scored higher 95% of the time on dev, 90% on val and 92% on test (the AUROC). Where they failed was calibration: urgent messages often got low probabilities, so the frozen thresholds of 5%, 20% and 50% had a recall of only 50% on dev, 69% on val and 51% on test. A third to a half of urgent messages were let wait.

A calibration curve shows the gap directly. Group the test messages by the baseline’s highest can’t-miss probability and check how many of each group the physician labelled urgent. If the probabilities were calibrated, the dots would sit on the diagonal. They sit far above it. Messages given a 10% to 29% chance were urgent 73% of the time (27 of 37), and every message given 30% or more was urgent (39 of 39). The model knew which messages were worse, but it stated their risk far too low.

No other cutoff fixes that. Recall is the share of urgent messages escalated; precision is the share of escalated messages that were urgent. Lowering the cutoff until recall matches the urgency screen’s drops precision to 86% on dev, 73% on val and 84% on test, even with an ideal cutoff, tuned on each set’s own answers. The screen keeps it at 97%, 85% and 93%. Recalibrating the scores, with Platt scaling for example, would not help either: it brings the probabilities in line with how often messages are actually urgent, but keeps their order, so its best threshold is already a point on this curve.

How the prompt was developed

The screen’s instructions were written and revised on two sets of messages, and frozen before the test set was ever run.

Dev is 127 messages: PMR-Bench’s own development split, plus 40 urgent messages from its training split so there were enough urgent cases to tune against. Val is 60 more messages from the training split, held back to check each revision. Three rules governed the work: no example in the prompt may come from a message used for evaluation, no more than three rounds of revision, and limits that dev and val both had to meet before the prompt could be frozen.

  1. Round 1: a first draft, run on dev

    The screen caught urgent messages but escalated far too much. Reading its reasons showed why: most of the extra escalations needed a visit, a test or a prescription, just not within three days. The draft never said where that line was.

  2. Round 2: the line, written down

    The revision added an explicit section on what cannot wait and what can, the lists shown above, and trimmed rules that overlapped with it. Run on dev again, it met every limit.

  3. Checked on val, then frozen

    On val it met both limits, so the prompt was frozen at round 2, with unsure messages escalated. Only then was the test set run, once.

Because val was looked at after each round, it is partly tuned on too. That is why the test set, which was never consulted during development, is the result that counts.

Why unsure has to escalate

Bar length is how often the screen gave each answer on the test set. 12 of the 14 messages it was unsure about were urgent: a worrying symptom, with the one fact that would decide it missing. Escalating unsure was decided on development data, before the test set was scored.

It holds on every set

Each dot is the rate, each line its Wilson 95% interval. The prompt was tuned on dev with val checked after each round, so both are partly tuned on; test is the result. Urgent misses stay near zero on all three.

Two seconds to escalate

An escalation is decided by the screen alone, so it never waits for the slower call. The screen costs $0.0048 a call and the judgment $0.0185; together they average $0.0095 a message. Every call is cached on its model, prompt, message, budget and effort, so every number on this page reproduces offline.

What it got wrong

13 of the 100 test messages went somewhere the physician would not have sent them. Most err toward caution; three do not.

The screen reasoned that the pill works best early and escalated it. The automated reply could have answered at once; the urgency was real, but it did not need a clinician.

Queuesent toUrgent
A head injury in a car crash, already scanned

The hospital’s CT was clear and she had been discharged with return precautions. The screen weighed her ongoing symptoms over the assessment that had already happened.

A question about antibiotic ear drops

A safe-to-wait medication question the judgment kept for a clinician, because the right answer depended on findings it could not see. Five self-care messages went this way: cautious, and the commonest error.

Self-caresent toUrgent
Diarrhea an hour after a suspect meal

She had cooked with a carton of broth whose seal turned out to be broken, and an hour later she and her partner both had cramps and diarrhea. The screen could not tell ordinary food poisoning from something worse and answered unsure, so it was escalated. The physician would have sent self-care guidance with return precautions. This is the price of escalating every unsure answer: a few messages like this one, for 12 urgent messages caught.

Failure modes

Every routing error from development and test, 37 in all, sorted by what went wrong and reviewed by the physician who labelled them. Dev and val errors count alongside the test set’s 13 so that eight modes have enough cases behind them; the solid part of each bar is the test set.

The under-triage errors are about exposures and absences. The first mode needs no clinical reasoning to block: a policy rule that an exposure question never goes to self-care would have stopped the worst error on the test set without touching the prompt. The second is the screen’s own rule, that silence is not reassurance, failing to hold.

Over-escalation is the price of the safety margin. A prompt revision aimed at the first two over-escalation modes was tried and rejected: urgent misses rose from 3 to 11 on dev and from 2 to 6 on val. Any rule that loosens the screen cuts into the urgent cases first. The time-sensitive mode points somewhere else: a route that sends the self-care reply at once and still puts the message in front of a physician.

Over-referrals are the cheapest errors. They cost physician time, not safety. Most are questions that mention a test or a medication, which the judgment reads as needing a clinician even when standard guidance would answer them.

The pattern behind the misses

The adversarial set includes cases where the fact that decides the route is missing. One of them:

“I’ve had a fever of 100.8 since yesterday and I’m achy all over. Do I need to be seen?”

With no age or immune status, the worst case behind that gap is sepsis in someone on chemotherapy, and a system that cannot ask has to resolve upward. The screen listed the warning signs that were absent and let it wait; the judgment cleared it to self-care. One of the two urgent misses on the test set has the same shape. The other is the bat scratch: the risk was stated, and the screen discounted it.

28 cases written from property descriptions and adjudicated before the run, two per property. The screen held 15 of the 16 that must escalate, including heart-attack and stroke symptoms paraphrased into everyday words and misspelled in text-speak, and neither prompt-injection case changed a route.

Limits

  • One physician’s labels. Every headline number is agreement with one person’s reading of a three-day line. A second labeller would bound what any system can score.
  • The benchmark’s own levels disagree. PMR-Bench calls 40 of the test messages urgent; the physician calls 70. Its labels are an LLM’s reading of an expert’s reply, not a triage policy with a three-day queue behind it, so they are reported alongside, never instead.
  • Small groups. 30 non-urgent and 13 self-care messages in the test set; one message moves the escalation rate by more than three points.
  • Reddit is not a patient portal. Posts are longer, written to strangers and often after the fact.
  • The message is all it sees. No chart, no history and no follow-up question. One of the two test-set misses turns on a fact the patient did not give.
  • The cost is escalation. At a realistic 18% urgent mix, about 31% of messages would escalate and about one escalation in two would be truly urgent. Whether the urgent route can absorb that is a question for the service, not the model.
